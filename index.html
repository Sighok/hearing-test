Tone Threshold Test

Find the quietest tone you can hear at each frequency, then compare frequencies. The page can't know how loud your headphones are, so levels are in dB below digital full scale (dBFS). Differences between frequencies are still meaningful as long as you leave your volume alone.

Set your volume

Use wired headphones in a quiet room. Turn off noise cancelling, spatial audio, EQ and "loudness" effects. Play the reference tone and set your device volume so it sounds soft but clear. Don't change the volume again until you've tested every frequency.

Reading the numbers

  • Threshold is the quietest level you heard on at least 2 rising presentations (the Hughson–Westlake method audiologists use). More negative means you heard a quieter tone.
  • Δ vs reference is how many dB more (+) or less (−) signal you needed at that frequency compared with the reference. For example, +6 dB at 500 Hz means 500 Hz had to be 6 dB louder than 1000 Hz before you heard it.
  • Typical Δ is the same difference for an average young listener with normal hearing, from the ISO 226:2003 hearing threshold curve (free field). Normal ears are less sensitive at low and very high frequencies, so a non-zero Δ is expected.
  • Your headphones colour the result. Their frequency response adds directly to every Δ, often by ±5 dB or more, and repeat tests normally vary by about ±5 dB. Retest each frequency two or three times and look at the average. This is not a medical test. See an audiologist if you're worried about your hearing.
